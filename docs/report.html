01

The problem

A robot told “put the mug beside the laptop” faces a decision most autonomy stacks skip: it can only act well if it actually knows which mug, whether it can see it, and whether reaching it is safe. A system that always acts is fast but reckless; a system that always asks is safe but exhausting to supervise. The interesting behavior lives in between — knowing when the uncertainty is worth a human’s attention.

This project studies that decision. Given uncertainty about a tabletop manipulation task, a learned policy chooses one of three actions:

ACT

Execute autonomously. Chosen when the command is unambiguous, the target is clearly perceived, and the path is safe.

ASK

Request one clarification, then execute. Chosen when a cheap question resolves the ambiguity — e.g. two mugs match “the mug”.

DEFER

Hand the task to a human. Chosen when execution itself is unsafe — a fragile object sits in the reach path, or the target is unreachable.

The distinction between ASK and DEFER is the crux: asking resolves what to do, deferring concedes that doing it at all is the risk.

02

Method — risk-aware selective autonomy

The decision reduces to expected-cost minimization. In simulation the true world is known, so an oracle computes the cost of each action and labels the cheapest one — no human annotation required:

Cost model — an unsafe collision is the failure to avoid
ActionExpected costWins when…
ACT punsafe·3 + (1−punsafe)(1−pcorrect)·1 safe & confident
ASK 0.1 + punsafe·3 safe but ambiguous
DEFER 0.3 execution is dangerous

Costs: unsafe collision = 3.0, wrong object = 1.0, defer = 0.3, ask = 0.1. Asking fixes the target’s identity, not the physical danger — so for hazardous scenes DEFER strictly beats ASK.

What the policy sees

The policy never sees the truth — only a 10-dimensional feature vector: five noisy uncertainty signals plus scene statistics. The signals are u_language (referential ambiguity), u_perception (object identification), u_planning (reach difficulty), u_ood (unfamiliarity), and u_safety (predicted collision risk). A tiny MLP maps them to a decision.

Five policies compared

Two trivial baselines (always act, always ask), a hand-tuned fixed-threshold rule, a learned classifier over the oracle action, and the headline method — a cost-sensitive network that regresses the three expected costs and picks the argmin, reasoning about how bad each mistake would be rather than where a boundary sits. Every result below is the mean over 5 random seeds.

03

Research questions

RQ1  Can uncertainty-aware selective autonomy cut task failures while asking for far less help than an always-ask policy?
RQ2  Which uncertainty signal matters most for the decision?
RQ3  Does calibrated uncertainty keep the robot safe under distribution shift?

RQ2 · Which signal matters

Retraining with one signal removed answers this cleanly. Dropping u_safety is the most damaging — it more than doubles the unsafe rate (1.5% → 3.8%). The unfamiliarity signal u_ood is a clear second (unsafe 1.5% → 1.8%), because an out-of-distribution object is riskier to grasp. The ambiguity/perception/planning signals refine the cheaper ACT-vs-ASK boundary and move the headline safety metrics much less — an honest, interpretable ordering given the cost model is dominated by the price of a collision.

RQ3 · Calibration under distribution shift

In-distribution the policy is almost perfectly calibrated; under shift its confidence becomes measurably less reliable (expected calibration error rises ~20×). Rather than fail, it responds the way the design intends — asking and deferring more, raising human effort by 34 points while holding the unsafe rate near 1.4%. Uncertainty is converted into help requests, not mistakes.

The cost model is the risk dial

Because decisions minimize expected cost, the price assigned to an unsafe collision is the operator’s risk setting. Sweeping it traces the full autonomy/safety spectrum: cheap-failure regimes act often; costly-failure regimes defer often and drive collisions toward zero.

Transfer to real robot scenes

The same trained policy — never exposed to a physics simulator during training — was evaluated on real ManiSkill scenes: a Panda arm, colored cubes for referential ambiguity, and a fragile object as the hazard, rendered on an NVIDIA T4 GPU. It transfers cleanly, and human effort scales with difficulty exactly as designed.

05

Discussion & limitations

What holds. A single risk-aware policy reaches near-always-ask success with a fraction of the human effort and roughly a quarter of the unsafe actions, transfers to a real robot simulator, and its behavior is tunable end-to-end through one interpretable cost. Results are stable across seeds (std ≤ 0.01).

What is deliberately simulated — and why the seam is real. The five uncertainty signals are modeled, not produced by real perception and planning models. To show this is a staged design rather than a limitation baked into the results, a small network was trained to estimate the collision probability from the other observations (no access to the true safety signal); it recovers the true risk with correlation 0.87, and the full policy runs on that learned estimate in place of the simulated one. A real detector or motion planner can be dropped in the same way, without touching the policy, cost model, or metrics — the ManiSkill backend already reuses the exact decision code on real scene geometry.

Next. Replace each simulated signal with its real estimator; add closed-loop grasp execution so ManiSkill reports true task success on ACT (scaffolded); and scale the scene complexity toward cluttered, multi-object tabletops.
