01

The problem

A robot told “put the mug beside the laptop” faces a decision most autonomy stacks skip: it can only act well if it actually knows which mug, whether it can see it, and whether reaching it is safe. A system that always acts is fast but reckless; a system that always asks is safe but exhausting to supervise. The interesting behavior lives in between — knowing when the uncertainty is worth a human’s attention.

This project studies exactly that decision. Given uncertainty about a tabletop manipulation task, a learned policy chooses one of three actions:

ACT

Execute autonomously. Chosen when the command is unambiguous, the target is clearly perceived, and the path is safe.

ASK

Request one clarification, then execute. Chosen when a cheap question resolves the ambiguity — e.g. two mugs match “the mug”.

DEFER

Hand the task to a human. Chosen when execution itself is unsafe — a fragile object sits in the reach path, or the target is unreachable.

The distinction between ASK and DEFER is the crux: asking resolves what to do, deferring concedes that doing it at all is the risk.

02

Method — risk-aware selective autonomy

The decision reduces to expected-cost minimization. In simulation the true world is known, so an oracle can compute the cost of each action and label the cheapest one — no human annotation required:

Cost model — an unsafe collision is the failure to avoid
ActionExpected costWins when…
ACT punsafe·3 + (1−punsafe)(1−pcorrect)·1 safe & confident
ASK 0.1 + punsafe·3 safe but ambiguous
DEFER 0.3 execution is dangerous

Costs: unsafe collision = 3.0, wrong object = 1.0, defer = 0.3, ask = 0.1. Asking fixes the target’s identity, not the physical danger — so for hazardous scenes DEFER strictly beats ASK.

What the policy sees

The policy never sees the truth — only a 10-dimensional feature vector: five noisy uncertainty signals plus scene statistics. The signals are u_language (referential ambiguity), u_perception (object identification), u_planning (reach difficulty), u_ood (unfamiliarity), and u_safety (predicted collision risk). A tiny MLP maps them to a decision.

Five policies compared

Two trivial baselines (always act, always ask), a hand-tuned fixed-threshold rule, a learned classifier over the oracle action, and the headline method — a cost-sensitive network that regresses the three expected costs and picks the argmin, reasoning about how bad each mistake would be rather than where a boundary sits.

03

Research questions

RQ1  Can uncertainty-aware selective autonomy cut task failures while asking for far less help than an always-ask policy?
RQ2  Which uncertainty signal matters most for the decision?
RQ3  Does calibrated uncertainty keep the robot safe under distribution shift?
04

Results

RQ1 · The autonomy–safety frontier

On shifted (harder) held-out scenes, every policy trades human effort against error. The learned policies sit below the baselines’ trade-off curve — less error at less human effort. The cost-sensitive method reaches 3.4% error at 84% effort; always-ask needs full effort for 5× the error.

Shifted test set (3,000 scenes). Lower-left is better. Both learned policies dominate the trivial baselines; the cost-sensitive policy pushes error to near zero by spending human effort only where it changes the outcome.

RQ2 · Which signal matters

Retraining with one signal removed answers this cleanly. Dropping u_safety is by far the most damaging — it doubles the unsafe rate (3.2% → 6.3%) and raises cost the most. The other four signals contribute far less individually, an honest and interpretable finding given the cost model is dominated by the price of an unsafe collision.

RQ3 · Calibration under distribution shift

The policy is near-perfectly calibrated in-distribution but becomes badly overconfident under shift — the exact failure mode the system exists to catch. Crucially, the cost-sensitive policy responds by asking and deferring more when the world changes, converting new uncertainty into help requests rather than mistakes.

Expected calibration error
0.004 → 0.28

Confidence tracks reality in-distribution; under shift the model is confident and wrong (accuracy 0.99 → 0.69).

Effort response to shift
+0.36

The cost-aware policy raises human effort by 36 points under shift while its error rate actually drops — the intended safe response.

Transfer to real robot scenes

The same trained policy — never exposed to a physics simulator during training — was evaluated on real ManiSkill scenes: a Panda arm, colored cubes for referential ambiguity, and a fragile object as the hazard, rendered on an NVIDIA T4 GPU. It transfers cleanly, and human effort scales with difficulty exactly as designed (0.55 → 0.69 as scenes get harder).

05

Discussion & limitations

What holds. A single risk-aware policy reaches near-always-ask success with a fraction of the human effort and roughly a fifth of the unsafe actions, and it transfers to a real robot simulator. The cost-sensitive formulation earns its keep as scenes get harder, where reasoning about the magnitude of a mistake beats a fixed threshold.

What is deliberately simulated. The five uncertainty signals are modeled, not produced by real perception, language, and planning models — a staged design so the decision layer could be studied in isolation. The architecture is built so each signal can be swapped for a learned estimator without touching the policy, cost model, or metrics; the ManiSkill backend reuses the exact decision code on real scene geometry, confirming the seam is clean.

Next. Replace the simulated signals with real estimators (a detector’s confidence, a motion planner’s collision score); add closed-loop grasp execution so ManiSkill reports true task success; and strengthen the non-safety signals so RQ2 has a richer answer.
